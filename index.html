Inside a Hurricane: Seeing Hurricane Ian Through Multiple Satellite Bands

How do different GOES satellite channels reveal the evolution and structure of Hurricane Ian before landfall, and what specific weather patterns highlight its formation?

Zach Ong, May Wang, and Yimeng Wei

Hook

While formal science education is required in much of the country, one of the lesser covered subjects in current time has been climate. Many are underinformed as to how different weather anomalies develop, with the genercal public soley relying on weather channels to guide them without fully understanding the indicators that drive these extreme weather events. Using the GOES satellite imagery, we hope our visualization illustrates the structural evolution and atmospheric matterns behind Hurricane Ian's formation. By stressing upon these indicators, we aim to help communities, like those located in southeast United States, better better anticipate hurricanes and mitigate their impact through a more independent persepective.

What have we done so far?

So far, we have built an interactive map and wind-speed timeline using the National Hurricane Center's best-track data for Hurricane Ian. Readers can select a point, move a time slider, or play the timeline to explore how Ian's location and intensity changed. We have also added a side-by-side viewer for five existing GOES-16 satellite products, allowing readers to compare different observations of the storm. In addition, we embedded a NOAA infrared animation showing Ian's changing cloud pattern from September 25 to 28, 2022. Our local satellite images currently cover only day 272, and we still need to verify their exact observation times and channel information before drawing precise comparisons.

What will be the most challenging of your project to design and why?

The most challenging part will be connecting the satellite observations and the storm's changing intensity into a clear story that readers can explore. The satellite products measure different quantities and use different color scales, so we need to explain what each view shows without suggesting that its colors can be directly compared with another product. We also need to obtain and process images from matched locations and times so that readers can make meaningful comparisons across channels and across the storm's development. Another challenge is showing how changes in cloud structure relate to the best-track data without implying that an image alone can determine wind speed. Clear annotations, consistent scales, and guided prompts will help readers understand both the patterns and the limits of the data.
